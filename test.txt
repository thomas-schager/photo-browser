The old lighthouse had stood on the rocky promontory for over two centuries, its weathered stone walls absorbing the salt spray and the relentless battering of Atlantic storms. Fishermen who passed it at dusk claimed they could sometimes see a faint amber glow in the lantern room, even though the light had been decommissioned decades ago when the automated buoy system rendered it obsolete.

Clara had inherited the lighthouse from a great-uncle she had never met. The solicitor's letter arrived on a Tuesday in November, dense with legal language that she had to read three times before it made any sense. She drove out the following weekend, half expecting a ruin, and found instead a building that had been quietly, meticulously maintained by someone — or something — that left no visible trace.

The interior smelled of beeswax and dried thyme. A cast-iron stove in the kitchen corner held a faint warmth, as though a fire had burned there within the last few hours. On the wooden table sat a chipped enamel mug, a coil of rope, and a tide almanac opened to the current month, the relevant dates circled in pencil.

Clara stood in the doorway for a long time, listening to the wind work its way around the building, finding every gap and singing a different note through each one. Below, the sea moved with patient indifference, pulling and releasing the stones at the base of the cliff.

She climbed the spiral staircase slowly, trailing one hand along the cold iron rail. Each step groaned beneath her weight. At the top, the lantern room offered a panoramic view of grey water stretching to a horizon that blurred into sky. She pressed her palm flat against the glass and felt the vibration of the wind pushing back.

She decided, without quite knowing why, that she would stay.
